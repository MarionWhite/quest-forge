# Continent shapes: what makes them memorable

## 1. Principles

- **Make the coast fractal and give it a direction.** Coastlines look similar at every scale (Mandelbrot, *How Long Is the Coast of Britain?*, Science 1967). Every bay should carry smaller bays. But the roughness must line up with a structure: smooth beaches on soft or sandy coasts, ragged fjords and rias on hard or glaciated ones. Coasts alternate between bays and headlands, with hard rock forming the headlands and soft rock eroding into bays ([Exilian realistic-worlds class](https://exilian.co.uk/forum/index.php?msg=53254)). Beaches should be occasional smooth curves against a rough hard coast ([Fantastic Maps](https://www.fantasticmaps.com/?p=2195)).
- **Let geology drive the shape.** Sketch plates and mountain ranges first, then grow the land around them. A chain of islands reads as a mountain range running into the sea ([Codidact: coastline tips](https://scientific-speculation.codidact.com/q/230303); [Pathfinder GM guide](https://aonprd.com/Rules.aspx?ID=720)). Most "blobs" are coastlines drawn with no skeleton underneath.
- **Avoid perfect shapes.** Square continents, sharp corners and even edges are the most common mistakes. A good shortcut is to trace a real coastline and distort it, which keeps a natural amount of wiggle ([Cartographers' Guild](https://www.cartographersguild.com/showthread.php?p=213476)).
- **Give it a recognisable silhouette at thumbnail size.** People remember 1–3 strong protrusions (a boot, a hook, a long arm) more than an even spread of detail. Make the shape top-heavy or lean it to one side. A centred shape with spikes all round produces the "tooth" or "starfish" look.
- **Peninsulas and gulfs make the interest.** They create natural chokepoints, borders, quest destinations and "end of the world" tips. A long, narrow gulf also lets the sea reach deep inland, which gives the interior a coastline.
- **Avoid the donut.** An inland sea surrounded by an even ring of land reads as artificial. If the centre is water, as in our crater lake, make it off-centre and connected to the sea, or ringed by mountains of uneven heights.
- **Make climate follow the shape.** Rain shadows sit behind mountains, deserts sit leeward or around 30° latitude, and cold currents lie offshore of west-coast fog belts (California's redwoods). A believable climate layout makes the shape feel deliberate. Don't cram too many biomes into too little area ([Vocal: real-world geography for D&D](https://vocal.media/gamers/real-world-geography-hacks-for-building-believable-d-and-d-worlds)).
- **Size detail to the viewing scale.** Detail below what players will ever see is wasted work ([ProFantasy forum](https://forum.profantasy.com/discussion/comment/12928/)). At 49k blocks, coastal features of roughly 200–2,000 blocks are what players actually notice.

## 2. Reference shapes

1. **Baja and the Gulf of California.** A 1,200 km arm split from the mainland by a rift, leaving a narrow sea between parallel coasts. This is the direct model for our gulf: a desert canyon on the peninsula and volcanic islands inside the gulf.
2. **Elden Ring, the Lands Between.** Each region sits on a plateau separated from the next by cliffs and lakes, so the map reads as layered tiers with the Erdtree as a landmark everywhere. Take the vertical layering and the single central beacon (our crater volcano).
3. **Tamriel and Skyrim.** A lopsided continent with two deep bays (the Topal and Iliac Bays) cut into its southern half. It shows how bays create provinces.
4. **Westeros.** Long and narrow, with a cold north, a wall-like barrier and a hot south. The shape tells the story from north to south. The weakness is that it is visibly a flipped Britain and Ireland.
5. **Middle-earth.** The mountains (Misty Mountains, Ered Lithui) shape the regions more than the coast does. Its west coast with gulfs (the Gulf of Lhûn and the Bay of Belfalas) is worth borrowing.
6. **Kalimdor.** A tall north–south spine with a tundra-and-forest north, deserts in the south (Tanaris) and a canyon (Thousand Needles). It shows climate bands running along a long axis.
7. **Scandinavia.** A curved arm with a mountain spine and fjords on its windward west coast. A model for our cold north and caldera highlands.
8. **Kamchatka.** A hanging peninsula lined with volcanoes, with calderas and crater lakes such as Kurile Lake. An ideal reference for our crater-lake and glacial-caldera zones.
9. **Italy.** The boot is the gold standard for a recognisable silhouette: a single mountain spine (the Apennines) with a gulf in the heel (the Gulf of Taranto).
10. **The Peloponnese.** Three fingers hang off a narrow isthmus, so its silhouette is three fingers rather than a single tooth. Good for one heavily indented corner of the map.
11. **South Island, New Zealand.** A single alpine spine, a wet fjorded west coast and a dry rain-shadowed east. A ready template for the redwood strip and the rain shadow.
12. **Iceland.** A roughly round body made characterful by the Westfjords "hand" and by its glaciers and calderas. It shows that one strong appendage rescues a blob.
13. **Sulawesi.** Four long arms around deep gulfs. A bold shape, but it slides toward "starfish" if you copy it whole. Use it only for one corner.
14. **Madagascar and Florida.** Smooth, low-indent shapes. They show the blobby end of the range, and are mainly a warning.

## 3. Five archetypes (each with a southern gulf)

**A. The Hook (Baja-led).** A broad northern shoulder of tundra and a glacial caldera sweeps down into one long southern arm, with a rift gulf between the arm and the mainland. A coastal range runs the whole west side, putting redwoods on its windward slope and the desert canyon in its rain shadow on the arm. The crater lake sits where the shoulder narrows into the arm.

**B. The Spine (Italy and New Zealand).** A long diagonal landmass running NW to SE, with one central mountain chain. The west slope holds the redwoods and the east slope holds dry plains and canyon country. The north end splays into a tundra plateau around the glacial caldera, and the south end forks into two prongs around a long gulf. The crater-lake volcano sits on the spine at its midpoint.

**C. The Crescent (Kamchatka and Japan).** A wide arc curving east around a volcanic interior sea. The crater lake is inland but off-centre, ringed by peaks of different heights and joined to the ocean by a canyon cut. Tundra and the caldera fill the thick northern horn, redwoods line the outer western curve, and the southern horn tapers into a peninsula enclosing the gulf with desert on its inner shore.

**D. The Two Lobes (Kalimdor-like).** A large northern body of tundra, glacial caldera and taiga joins a smaller southern body through a narrow isthmus guarded by volcanic ground and the crater lake. The southern lobe is desert and canyon, and its far tip splits into a long gulf. A western cordillera runs the full length, with redwoods along its seaward side.

**E. The Fjord Slab (Scandinavia and Iceland).** A broad, top-heavy block whose northern and western coasts are shredded by fjords. These hold the glacial caldera and the tundra, with redwoods in the sheltered southwestern fjord valleys. The east coast is smooth and low. The body tapers south-east to a single point with a long gulf behind it, and the desert canyon is cut into the dry interior plateau. The crater lake rises out of the central highlands.
